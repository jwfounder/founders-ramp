Demo like a builder, not a tour guide

Show the buyer their own problem getting solved. Skip the rest.

In short

A demo is a non-fiction story of the buyer's future, built only from what they told you in discovery. Open on their problem, show only what discovery showed is broken, check in every five minutes, prove it with something real, including an edge case, and leave ten minutes to agree where to start. Everything else is a tour.

Picture your buyer's week. They're in an active evaluation, so by Thursday they've sat through a stack of slide decks: the market slide, the logo wall, the architecture diagram, the login screen, the home dashboard every vendor in the category seems to have. Then you join the call and share your screen.

If your first ten minutes look like the rest of that stack, you've lost the room. Not because the product is weak. Because the buyer has stopped looking for a reason to change and started comparing features.

The fix is easy to say and hard to do. Show the product working on a real problem the buyer already told you about, within minutes, and stop touring every screen. We call it demoing like a builder instead of a tour guide. A tour guide walks you through every room in the building. A builder asks what you need, then shows you the room built for it.

Discovery earns the demo

The demo starts on the call before it. If the first invite says "demo," you've told the buyer their job is to watch. We'd rather the first invite say introductory discussion, and spend that hour learning what is broken, who it hurts, and what it costs when it stays broken. If the conversation goes well, the demo is the next call, built from what you heard.

That one change shifts everything after it. You're no longer demonstrating software or proving how big your feature set is. You're helping a buyer build a case for a change inside their company, and the demo is the evidence.

Founders skip this step because the product is the thing they're proudest of. We get it. But a demo with no discovery behind it is a guess with a screen share.

Only show what discovery showed is broken

In one demo we sat in on, a buyer said early on: "We would just focus on item eight." They had a list of problems, and they told the team which one mattered. The team ran the full tour anyway, items one through seven first, in order. Near the end, someone else on the buyer's side said nothing they'd seen was life changing compared with what they do today.

Nobody on that call was wrong about the product. The demo answered questions nobody asked, and it ran down the clock before it got to the one they did.

When a buyer narrows the list, that's the most useful thing they'll say all hour. Let them pull you to what hurts. Go straight to item eight, show it working on their kind of data, and stop.

Before the call, list every screen you could show, then cross off anything that doesn't connect to a problem the buyer named. The crossed-off screens are still there if someone asks. You just don't lead with them.

Then open on their problem, in their words, not on the home screen they've already seen all week. "You told us your team rebuilds the same report by hand every month. Here's that report, built from the source, in about a minute." That earns more attention than any amount of navigation.

"Every deal is a different conversation"

We hear this from a lot of founders, usually as the reason their demo can't be repeatable. Every buyer is different, so every demo is different, so the whole thing is improvised.

It's half true. In the demos we review, a small part stays the same from call to call: how you open, how you check in, how you handle price, how you end. Most of it should change, because it comes from what this buyer said in discovery.

Most founders get it backwards. They improvise the part that should stay constant, so the open wanders, the check-ins never happen, and the call ends with "we'll send something over." And they freeze the part that should change, so every buyer gets the same seven-item tour.

Flip it. Keep the frame the same every time, and let discovery fill it in. The frame is short:

  • Open on the problem they described, with their numbers if you have them.
  • Show two or three moments, each one tied to something they said.
  • Check in about every five minutes.
  • Leave ten minutes at the end.

The moments inside the frame are where every deal really is a different conversation. That part should feel built for this buyer, because it was.

You said, we showed, do you see

Each moment has three parts. Start with the buyer's own words: "You said approvals sit in email for a week." Show the product handling exactly that. Then ask one question: do you see how that would work for you?

Not "isn't that great." The buyer already assumes you think your software is great. What you need to know is whether they can picture it working in their environment, with their team, next quarter. When the buyer explains it back in their own words, that's the part that sells. It's also what they'll repeat to their boss when you're not in the room.

This is why we split the demo between two people whenever we can. It's very hard to drive the screen and run the conversation at the same time. While you're clicking, you aren't watching faces, and the buyer isn't talking. One person drives. The seller opens each moment with what the buyer said and closes it with the question. The seller's job on a demo is to get the buyer talking about what they just saw and what it means.

Check in every five minutes

We went through one demo recording minute by minute. At 27 minutes, a buyer said, "I don't understand exactly what you're saying." The presenter kept going. The first real check-in came at 38 minutes. That's eleven minutes of talking to someone who had already said they were lost.

Now we time it. A short question about every five minutes, in plain words. Is this the part you'd change? What would this mean for your team? Does this match how it works today? If the answer is no, you just saved twenty minutes of the wrong demo.

Prove it with something real

Near the end, the buyer needs a reason to believe this works outside your demo environment. A logo wall won't do it. One true story will: a customer that looked like them, the problem they had, and what changed. Keep it specific, keep it true, and leave the names out if you have to. A team about their size, with the same approval mess, that no longer needs the spreadsheet is enough, as long as it actually happened.

Then do the harder thing. Show what happens when the real account doesn't look like the demo.

Every buyer knows their data is messier than your sample data. They have the duplicate records, the exception nobody wrote up, the one customer billed three different ways. If your demo only ever runs the clean path, the sharpest person in the room is quietly assuming it breaks on theirs. So bring an edge case on purpose. Show the exception landing, show how it gets flagged, show who fixes it and what that takes. A product that handles one ugly case honestly is more believable than one that handles ten clean ones perfectly.

If they ask about something you haven't built, or a kind of customer you haven't served, say so. Tell them you have customers, but none quite like them yet, and book the follow-up. Over-claiming feels like it saves the deal in the moment. Then people on your side give different answers to the same question, and the buyer stops believing any of them.

Leave ten minutes

Most demos run to the last minute, and the next step gets squeezed into "we'll send something over." Stop with ten minutes left.

Play back what they told you they need, in their words, and ask if you got it right. Then ask where they'd want to start first: one workflow, one team, one problem. That question turns a presentation into a plan they helped make. Before anyone drops off, put a date on the next step, with an owner on their side and an owner on yours.

If you can't get a date, that's information too. Better to learn it at minute fifty than in week six.

Your next demo this week

Pick the next demo on your calendar. Go back to your discovery notes and find the one problem the buyer was most specific about. Open on that, in their words, with their numbers. Cut every screen that doesn't connect to something they said. Mark check-ins on your notes at minutes five, ten, fifteen and twenty. Stop at minute fifty.

After the call, check one thing: did the buyer talk more in the second half than in the first?

Your next demo: the whole tour, or the one item they named?

Common questions

What should a founder open a demo with?

The problem the buyer described in discovery, in their words and with their numbers if you have them. Not the login screen, the logo wall or the home dashboard they have already seen from other vendors.

How much of the product should I show?

Only what connects to a problem the buyer named. List every screen you could show, cross off anything that doesn't connect, and keep the rest ready in case someone asks.

Can a demo be repeatable if every deal is different?

Yes. A small part stays the same on every call: the open, the check-ins, how you handle price and how you end. The larger part, the moments you show, comes from what this buyer said in discovery.

How often should I check in during a demo?

About every five minutes, with a short question in plain words. If a buyer says they are lost and you keep going, the rest of the demo is wasted.

How should a demo end?

Stop with ten minutes left. Play back what they need, ask where they would want to start first, and leave with a dated next step and an owner on each side.
